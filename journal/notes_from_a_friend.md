### Notes From a Friend


I’ve been living in this hotel for the last eight months. This morning, on my way up to our study room, I stepped into the elevator with a nice older man and started making conversation.

I had no idea who he was. I was just being friendly.

“Are you here for business?”

“Yes, I’m giving lectures at Herzliya Polytechnic.”

“Really? What are you a professor in?”

“Oh, you know,” he said casually. “What everyone does these days. Computer science.”

After that we chatted, he was funny. When I told him I was in the military, he said the government had been one of his greatest benefactors.

"Really? What department?"

“Well, actually, they were companies that didn’t exist.”

That got my attention lol.

Our conversation continued in the study room, the same room where I’ve been working through my own programming lessons for the OMSCS. Except now I was talking with Sergei Nirenburg, a professor at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute who has spent decades researching artificial intelligence.

It was a conversation I never thought I would have, especially here.

Let me try to paint the picture of what he’s working on.

Imagine trying to find the shortest route between two places. A brute-force approach would check every possible route and compare the distances. More sophisticated algorithms can avoid much of that work by using the structure of the problem and information to help guide the search.

That example helped me appreciate why the "how" a system solves a problem matters so much. It isn’t a direct description of how chatbots work, but it gave me a way into what he was explaining.

Much of today’s excitement around AI centers on large language models, like those behind ChatGPT and Claude, which learn patterns from enormous amounts of data. These models can have billions of parameters: numerical values learned during training that shape how they process information and generate responses.

Sergei told me that his framework, OntoAgent, works with only dozens of parameters. That contrast caught my attention.

As he explained it, his approach puts explicitly represented knowledge about the world at the center of an agent’s reasoning. The goal is to build agents that can use that knowledge to interpret situations, reason about goals, and decide what to do. His team’s robotics research brings that approach into the physical world, where decisions have consequences beyond words on a screen.

One of the hard parts is building and maintaining all that knowledge. His team is also researching ways language models can help with that process, which I thought was fascinating.

I can’t pretend I understood every technical detail, but I walked away excited by the possibility that his work could help change how autonomous robots interact with people and the world around them.

But the conversation wasn’t only about AI. I asked him a few career questions, and three things really stuck with me.

First to never stop loving what you are doing. "If you become sad because of your work, change and iterate." Your work should be able to help solve a problem in the world that you care about. 

That is totally a research level of thinking, industry is different due to financial pressures, customers and sorts but I think the similarities is that I need to look for the joy in working to solve problems, not specifically finding joy in solving a problem. Growth mindset and all

Second, early in your career, explore many different ways of solving problems.

For me, that means learning different approaches to machine learning and understanding when each one is useful. One model might be incredible for a particular problem. Another project might call for several models working together, alongside other kinds of software and reasoning.

In a perfect world, I’d love to build an intelligent system that keeps learning and solves everyday problems for people. That’s a dream of mine.

His advice made me want to look beyond the methods that happen to be popular in one industry. People in different fields have spent years figuring out different ways to solve problems. I want to learn enough to recognize when one of those ideas could help somewhere else, instead of only working inside the box an industry has already built.

Finally, he said to find the role you serve on a team and do it better than anyone else.

For him, that was system design. Literally something I would love to do lol.

He told me about a guy he worked with who was an incredible programmer. Sergei could design a system, and this guy would turn it into thousands of lines of working code, sometimes overnight.

But getting him to write down and explain what he had built was another story.

Sergei leaned in close, as if he were telling me a secret.

“I wrote half of his dissertations!”

Then he gave a little chuckle.

He told me he had stopped coding about 25 years ago and had focused instead on designing systems and leading teams of talented people. Hearing him describe it made that kind of work feel much more real to me. He had found where he could contribute most and built his career around it.

After all of that, I asked him about the accent I’d noticed.

“Where are you from?”

“Well, I teach at RPI.”

“No, what country is your accent from?”

“Ukraine.”

“Wow. You must have an incredible story, sir.”

“Story? Stories!”

Before we finished, he told me, “Never stop smiling.”

That stayed with me. Here was a man from a country enduring a devastating war, with a lifetime of stories I had barely begun to hear, telling me to never stop smiling.

Eight months in this hotel, and this morning an elevator ride turned into a conversation about AI, finding my place on a team, and enjoying the work of becoming good at something.

I thought I was just saying hello to a nice old man.

Instead, God gave me encouragement in the work I am persisting in studying. Thank you Jesus
